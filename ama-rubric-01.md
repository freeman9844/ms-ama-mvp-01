# Capstone Assessment Guide

Azure Master Architect Program | FY27 Cohort | For Participants

This is the complete rubric your panel will use. Nothing is held back — you are seeing the same criteria and the same descriptors the panelists score against. Read it early, not the week before.

Twenty criteria, four sections, 100 points. Every criterion is worth five points. Technical Excellence and Communication & Executive Presence are balanced at seven criteria each and together carry 70% of your score. That is deliberate. The bar for Master Architect is not "can design a system." It is "can design a system, land it with an executive sponsor, and leave the practice better than you found it."

## How the score is made up

| Section | Criteria | Points | Share | How it is scored |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A. Technical Excellence | 7 | 35 | 35% | Live, by the panel |
| B. Communication & Executive Presence | 7 | 35 | 35% | Live, by the panel |
| C. Consultative & Stakeholder Skills | 3 | 15 | 15% | Live, by the panel |
| D. Thought Leadership Artifact | 3 | 15 | 15% | Before panel day, by two assigned reviewers |
| Total | 20 | 100 | 100% | — |

## Section A — Technical Excellence (35 points)

### A1–A3. Design & Governance

| Criterion | Excellent (5) | Good (4) | Satisfactory (3) | Needs Improvement (1–2) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A1. Architecture, modularity & scalability | Clear, documented architecture; components decoupled with defined contracts; scaling path and failure domains explicit | Sound, modular architecture; scaling considered but not fully evidenced | Workable design; noticeable coupling; scaling addressed only in general terms | Monolithic or undocumented; no scaling consideration |
| A2. Trade-off reasoning & design patterns | Patterns chosen deliberately; names the alternatives considered and why they were rejected | Appropriate patterns; trade-offs stated but not fully argued | Patterns present but applied by default rather than by choice | Patterns absent or misapplied; no alternatives considered |
| A3. Security, compliance & responsible AI | Identity, data protection and isolation addressed against a stated threat model; compliance named; AI risks (grounding, injection, leakage) mitigated | Core controls in place; AI risk considered; threat model implied rather than stated | Basic controls only; AI risks acknowledged but not mitigated | Security and AI risk treated as an afterthought or omitted |

### A4–A7. Build, Operate & AI

| Criterion | Excellent (5) | Good (4) | Satisfactory (3) | Needs Improvement (1–2) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A4. Implementation completeness | All required capabilities working end to end; known gaps disclosed proactively | Core capabilities working; minor gaps | Partial implementation; key paths incomplete | Fragmentary or non-functional |
| A5. Operations — observability, reliability, performance & cost | Structured logging, metrics and traces; targets stated and measured; resilience patterns applied; cost modelled per unit of business value | Instrumented and resilient; cost or performance evidenced, but not both | Basic logging; operations and cost discussed qualitatively, nothing measured | No instrumentation; run-time behaviour and cost unaddressed |
| A6. AI solution design & evaluation | Model choice justified against alternatives on cost, latency and quality; evaluation harness with defined metrics and documented failure modes | Sound model choice with partial justification; evaluation present but narrow | Model chosen by default; testing anecdotal rather than measured | AI bolted on without clear purpose; no evaluation |
| A7. Agentic autonomy & orchestration | Agent acts autonomously within clear guardrails; handoff, reflection or state-graph patterns used deliberately and justified | Real autonomy demonstrated; coordination simple but sound | Largely a scripted flow presented as agentic; limited independent action | No meaningful agentic behaviour |

## Section B — Communication & Executive Presence (35 points)

Scored live during your session, including the demo.

| Criterion | Excellent (5) | Good (4) | Satisfactory (3) | Needs Improvement (1–2) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| B1. Narrative structure & argument | Clear arc — problem, stakes, options, recommendation; the conclusion follows from the evidence presented | Logical flow; the argument is present but has to be inferred in places | Sound content sequenced as a tour of the build rather than an argument | Rambling; audience left to assemble the point |
| B2. Audience calibration & register | Pitches depth to the room; moves between executive and technical registers without losing either audience | Mostly well pitched; occasional drops into unnecessary detail | One register throughout regardless of who is listening | Pitched so that a non-technical listener could not follow |
| B3. Demo & executive hook | Demo lands the "so what" in under two minutes, shows outcome rather than features, and recovers gracefully if something breaks | Clear demo of the use case; hook present but slow to arrive | Feature walkthrough; the audience has to infer why it matters | Demo absent or impossible to follow; no recovery attempted |
| B4. Q&A composure & depth under challenge | Answers go deeper than the prepared material; separates what is known from what is assumed; stays composed under pressure | Answers well; occasionally defensive or over-qualifies | Handles factual questions; thin once questions go past the deck | Deflects, bluffs, or cannot answer beyond prepared material |
| B5. Delivery craft — pace, presence & time discipline | Composed and engaging; paces to land the key points; finishes inside the slot with room for questions | Confident delivery; minor pacing or timing slips | Delivery flat or rushed; overruns and compresses the ending | Reads from slides or notes; runs out of time before the recommendation |
| B6. Written communication — deck & capstone documentation | Slides carry one idea each and standalone without narration; capstone documentation is complete and navigable | Clear materials; some slides overloaded or documentation thin in places | Materials serve the speaker rather than the reader; documentation minimal | Dense, unedited slides; no usable documentation |
| B7. Use of evidence & data storytelling | Claims backed by numbers the audience can act on; visuals chosen to make the point, not to display the data | Evidence presents and relevant; some claims asserted rather than shown | Data shown but not interpreted; audience left to draw the conclusion | Assertion without evidence; charts decorative or misleading |

## Section C — Consultative & Stakeholder Skills (15 points)

Section B asks whether your message landed. Section C asks how you behaved with the people in the room — during framing, during the body of the session, and especially during Q&A.

| Criterion | Excellent (5) | Good (4) | Satisfactory (3) | Needs Improvement (1–2) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| C1. Discovery & business framing | Names the real stakeholders, constraints and success measures; quantify the value at stake and the cost of inaction | Business context clear; value stated qualitatively | Requirements restated rather than interrogated | Solution presented with no business grounding |
| C2. Active listening & disagreement navigation | Answers the question asked; restates challenges accurately before responding; disagrees without dismissing, and concedes when the point is good | Listens well; occasionally answers the question they expected rather than the one asked | Hears the question but treats disagreement as something to survive rather than explore | Talks over or past the questioner; becomes defensive or capitulates entirely |
| C3. Influence & intellectual honesty | Makes a recommendation and defends it; surfaces risk unprompted; says "I don’t know" without losing credibility | Recommendations clearly; risk disclosure partial | Presents options without taking a position | Overclaims, or hides limitations until asked |

## Section D — Thought Leadership Artifact (15 points)

| Criterion | Excellent (5) | Good (4) | Satisfactory (3) | Needs Improvement (1–2) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| D1. Substance & originality | Original synthesis grounded in real engagement evidence; another architect could act on it on Monday | Useful and well-evidenced; largely a synthesis of known material | Accurate but restates published guidance | Thin, derivative, or unsupported |
| D2. Craft & publish-readiness | Structured, edited and cited; ready to publish or file with no rework | Solid draft; light editing needed | Ideas present; structure or clarity needs significant work | Notes rather than a finished piece |
| D3. Dissemination & reach | Published, filed or delivered to a real audience beyond the cohort; discoverable by architects outside the program | Accepted or scheduled for publication; approval in progress | Complete and submitted for publication, not yet accepted | Produced for the rubric only; no publication path identified |

### What you need to know about Section D

- You submit one artifact. This is a graduation requirement — a missing artifact is an incomplete, not a low score.
- It is due no later than two weeks before your panel day. Start it early; it is 15% of your score and it cannot be produced well in a weekend.
- It is reviewed before panel day by two assigned reviewers who are not on your panel. Their scores are averaged.
- Your panel receives the artifact itself five business days beforehand and may ask you about it. Be ready to defend its specifics.
- No format earns more points than another. A repository is worth exactly as much as a white paper.
- AI assistance is permitted, including in drafting. The insight, the engagement evidence and the recommendation must be yours, and you will be examined on them.

### Accepted formats

Choose the format that fits what you have to say. You will be judged on whether the artifact is good of its kind — not on whether you picked the right kind.

| Accepted format | What counts as a complete submission |
|---|---|
| White paper or architecture guidance | Roughly 2,000+ words, structured and sourced, reusable beyond the originating engagement |
| Technical blog post | Roughly 800+ words, published or formally submitted to Tech Community, the internal architecture library, or an external outlet |
| Reference implementation or accelerator | Working code in a repository with a README covering purpose, architecture, setup and known limitations |
| IP disclosure or patent filing | Submitted to the IP review process, with the disclosure document attached |
| Community or conference session | Delivered to an audience, with published materials plus written notes or a recording |
| Slide deck alone | Not sufficient on its own. A deck must be paired with written notes, a recording, or a delivered session — slides without narration do not stand alone as thought leadership. |

## How overlapping moments are scored

Some criteria draw on the same few minutes of your session. It is worth knowing how that is handled.

- If your demo breaks, the panel scores how you recover (B3). Your build is only marked down (A4) if the failure reveals a genuine implementation gap — not for a bad network or a flaky conference room.
- A single Q&A exchange is scored twice, on two different things: how good the answer was (B4), and whether you actually heard the question and handled the disagreement well (C2). A strong answer delivered over the top of the questioner scores high on one and low on the other.
- Your capstone documentation is scored in B6. Your thought leadership artifact is scored only in Section D. They are separate deliverables.

## How the outcome is determined

The floor check is applied first. A total score never overrides a floor failure.

### Step 1 — Floors

- Each section must reach 60% of its available points: A ≥ 21/35, B ≥ 21/35, C ≥ 9/15, D ≥ 9/15.
- No individual criterion may be scored 1.
- The thought leadership artifact must have been submitted.
- Number of required masterclasses attended.

These apply symmetrically. You cannot offset weak communication with technical depth, and you cannot offset weak architecture with presence.

### Step 2 — Outcome (only if all floors are cleared)

- Distinction (90–100) — Exceptional. Technical rigor and executive credibility both fully evident.
- Pass with Merit (78–89) — Strong across the board. Graduates with a named development area.
- Pass (66–77) — Meets the bar. Graduates with a development plan.
- Not Yet Met (below 66) — Does not meet the bar.

## How to prepare

The most common way strong engineers lose points here is not technical. It is presenting the build instead of making an argument.

- Structure the session as an argument: the problem, why it matters commercially, the options you considered, what you recommend, and what it costs. Not a tour of your architecture diagram.
- Rehearse the demo to under two minutes, and rehearse what you say if it breaks. Both are scored.
- Bring numbers. Cost per transaction, latency, evaluation scores, the value at stake. Assertions without evidence cap you at a 3 on B7.
- Know what you rejected and why. A2 and B4 are where candidates who only know their own design come unstuck.
- Time it. Overrunning compresses your recommendation, which is the part that matters most.
- Treat challenge in Q&A as a test of how you think, not an attack. Restating the challenge before you answer it is a specific behavior C2 rewards.
- "I don’t know, here is how I would find out" scores well. Bluffing scores 1–2.
- Start the artifact in the first half of the program, while the engagement evidence is still fresh.
